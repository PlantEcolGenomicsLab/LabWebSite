## The question

Grasses grow from the tropics to the tundra, in deserts and in wetlands. Again and again,
separate grass lineages have moved into the same kinds of extreme environments. Are those
repeated moves built on the same genetic changes, and if so, can we predict them?

## Our approach

- **Dense sampling across the family.** Genomes from more than 550 grass species spanning
  17 climate zones.
- **Mapping every species' environment.** Occurrence records combined with climate and soil
  data describe where each species lives, and ancestral reconstruction identifies independent
  transitions into extreme temperature, water and soil conditions.
- **Testing for convergence.** Phylogenetic mixed models ask which genomic features shift
  together with the environment across those independent transitions, while accounting for
  shared ancestry.
- **Reading gene activity with AI.** Protein and DNA language models (ESM2 and PlantCAD) score
  how intact each gene is directly from its sequence, without multiple sequence alignments.

## What we found

- **Genome-wide shifts.** Lineages entering similar environments shifted in the same
  directions in proteome-wide properties, such as the nitrogen-to-carbon balance and the
  biosynthetic cost of their proteins.
- **Recurring genes.** Several hundred genes were repeatedly associated with distinct axes of
  environmental variation, enriched for proteins working in extracellular and organellar
  compartments.

Together, these results suggest that grass adaptation is channeled by layered constraints at
genome-wide and gene-specific scales, which makes evolutionary trajectories partly predictable.
The same framework now underpins all of the lab's research directions.
