## Why niches

Where a species lives, and how far it can push into hot, dry, cold or waterlogged places, is the
outcome of its adaptation. Treating the habitat as a measurable trait lets us compare hundreds of
species at once, and find the lineages that independently colonized similar extremes: the
replicated experiments of evolution.

## What we have built

- **A habitat pipeline.** Species occurrence records combined with global climate and soil
  databases characterize the habitat of any species.
- **Environmental transitions.** {{< paper doi="10.64898/2026.06.01.729361" text="Across more than 550 grass species" >}}, ancestral reconstruction
  revealed many independent moves into extreme temperature, water and soil conditions, the
  foundation of our [convergence analyses](../innovation/).
- **Below-ground ecology.** {{< paper doi="10.1111/tpj.70319" text="In Andropogoneae grasses" >}} we linked species' rhizosphere nitrogen
  dynamics to their evolution, pointing to nitrogen-saving strategies for agriculture.

## Extending to more species

Southeast Asia is a hotspot of grass diversity, shaped by a uniquely complex geology:

- **Indochina**, geologically stable and connected to the mainland, is a refuge for ancient
  forest grasses and a corridor for tropical and monsoonal lineages.
- **Taiwan**, a young island uplifted about 6 million years ago, covers ~0.03% of Earth's land yet
  hosts about 2.6% of the world's grass species (around 290, including 46 endemics).
- **The Philippines**, a volcanic arc never joined to the continent, gained its grasses by wind,
  birds and human voyagers.
- **Sundaland and Wallacea** hold ancient Asian lineages and a mixing zone between Asian and
  Australian floras.

Yet this diversity is barely represented in genomic and biodiversity databases. The United
Kingdom has about half as many grass species as the Philippines but roughly ten times more
occurrence records. Our plans:

- **Sampling and sequencing** about 150 additional grass species native to Taiwan and Southeast
  Asia, from the field, botanical gardens and herbarium specimens.
- **Better occurrence data** for the region through fieldwork and digitization, with local
  institutions.
- **Beyond grasses**: other ecologically important but understudied families, such as orchids
  (Orchidaceae) and bromeliads (Bromeliaceae).

All genomic and biogeographic resources will be shared openly with the community.
