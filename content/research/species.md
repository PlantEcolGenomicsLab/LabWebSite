## Why Southeast Asia

Grasses arose about 100 million years ago in tropical Gondwana and have since spread across the
globe. Southeast Asia is a hotspot of their diversity, shaped by a uniquely complex geological
history:

- **Indochina**, geologically stable and connected to the mainland, is a refuge for ancient
  forest grasses and a corridor for tropical and monsoonal lineages.
- **Taiwan**, a young island uplifted about 6 million years ago, was repeatedly connected to and
  separated from the mainland. Despite covering only ~0.03% of Earth's land, it hosts about 2.6%
  of the world's grass species (around 290, including 46 endemics).
- **The Philippines**, a volcanic arc never joined to the continent, gained its grasses by wind,
  birds and human voyagers.
- **Sundaland and Wallacea** hold ancient Asian lineages and a mixing zone between Asian and
  Australian floras.

## The gap

This diversity is barely represented in genomic and biodiversity databases. Only a handful of
grass genomes come from Taiwanese material, and occurrence records are strongly biased toward
well-studied regions. The United Kingdom, for example, has about half as many grass species as
the Philippines but roughly ten times more occurrence records. Our power analyses show that adding
more independent lineages is one of the most effective ways to sharpen the search for adaptive
genes.

## What we are doing

- **Sampling.** Collecting native grasses in the field, from botanical gardens and from
  herbarium specimens, with a goal of about 150 additional species from Taiwan and Southeast Asia.
- **Sequencing.** Long-read (PacBio HiFi) or short-read genome assemblies, depending on
  sample quality and taxonomic position.
- **Occurrence data.** Fieldwork and digitization to improve where-species-live records for the
  region, in collaboration with local institutions.
- **Beyond grasses.** Once the framework is proven, extending it to other ecologically
  important but understudied families such as orchids (Orchidaceae) and bromeliads
  (Bromeliaceae).

All genomic and biogeographic resources will be shared openly with the community.
